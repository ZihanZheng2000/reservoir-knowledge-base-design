## KC-LP-EMR-01 — Flood Frequency Reduction Measures (45,000 cfs / 100-year target)

- Operational question: What flood-frequency-reduction commitment governs unanticipated floods exceeding 45,000 cfs at Glen Canyon Dam, and how is it achieved?
- Engineering dimension: Emergency Operations
- Consolidation type: repeated_fact
- Consolidated finding: Under the 1996 ROD's Flood Frequency Reduction Measures, the frequency of unanticipated floods exceeding 45,000 cfs is to be reduced to an average of once in 100 years, accomplished initially through the Annual Operating Plan process and eventually by raising Glen Canyon Dam's spillway gates 4.5 feet (EU-LP-OFF-008-05). The 2002 Report to Congress restates the identical commitment: the frequency of unanticipated flood flows exceeding 45,000 cfs will be reduced to no more than 1 year in 100 years as a long-term average, using the same two-phase approach -- Annual Operating Plan process, then a 4.5-foot spillway gate raise (EU-LP-OFF-009-07).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-OFF-008-05`, `EU-LP-OFF-009-07`
- Source coverage: Same flood-frequency-reduction commitment (threshold, target frequency, and two-phase mitigation approach) stated in the 1996 ROD and repeated verbatim in substance in the 2002 Report to Congress.
- Confidence: high

## KC-LP-EMR-02 — Conditions for Exceeding the 25,000 cfs Release Cap

- Operational question: Under what conditions may Glen Canyon Dam exceed the normal 25,000 cfs maximum release limit?
- Engineering dimension: Emergency Operations
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: Under the 2016 LTEMP FEIS, when very wet hydrologic conditions occur (average monthly release rate above 25,000 cfs), Glen Canyon Dam may exceed the 25,000 cfs cap but must release at a constant rate; the limit may also be exceeded to avoid spills, during flood releases, for approved experimental high-flow-experiment (HFE) releases, or to accommodate electrical emergencies (EU-LP-OFF-005-06). Consistent with this, the 2007 Biological Assessment states the 25,000 cfs maximum flow limit under the 1996 ROD and 1997 Operating Criteria may be exceeded for emergency conditions and during extreme hydrological conditions, and notes that separate emergency exception criteria also apply during normal operations (EU-LP-OFF-011-16).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-OFF-005-06`, `EU-LP-OFF-011-16`
- Source coverage: The 2016 LTEMP FEIS gives a specific enumerated list of exceedance conditions (wet hydrology, spills, floods, HFEs, electrical emergencies); the 2007 Biological Assessment gives a more general emergency/extreme-hydrology framing of the same rule. Read together they give a fuller picture than either alone.

## KC-LP-EMR-03 — Glen Canyon Dam Emergency Exception Criteria

- Operational question: What are the Emergency Exception Criteria that allow Glen Canyon Dam to deviate from normal operating rules during emergencies, and what situations do they cover?
- Engineering dimension: Emergency Operations
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: The Emergency Exception Criteria were established under Section 3 of the Glen Canyon Dam Operating Criteria (62 FR 9448, March 3, 1997) and continue unchanged under LTEMP Alternative D; when invoked, normal operations are suspended until the emergency has ended or WAPA has discharged its NERC responsibility regarding the emergency (EU-LP-OFF-003-09). The 1996 ROD establishes that these criteria allow WAPA to respond to various emergency situations consistent with its North American Electric Reliability Council (NERC) obligations, with exceptions to standard operating criteria during search-and-rescue situations, special studies and monitoring, dam and powerplant maintenance, and spinning reserves (EU-LP-OFF-008-06). GAO's 1997 assessment describes these deviations as usually lasting less than 4 hours, covering power and water emergencies such as insufficient generating capacity, restoration of the electrical system, or search-and-rescue operations (EU-LP-OFF-010-05). The underlying NERC standard requires a disturbance (sudden generation loss or transmission imbalance) be corrected within 10 minutes; Glen Canyon Dam's reserve capacity is also contractually used to restart tripped thermal powerplants and provide emergency shutdown power to nuclear powerplants, and responding to such electrical emergencies is exempt from 1996 ROD operating constraints (EU-LP-OFF-005-11).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-OFF-003-09`, `EU-LP-OFF-008-06`, `EU-LP-OFF-010-05`, `EU-LP-OFF-005-11`
- Source coverage: Four documents (1996 ROD, 1997 GAO report, 2016 LTEMP ROD, 2016 LTEMP FEIS Chapter 3) describe the same Emergency Exception Criteria instrument from different angles -- legal citation and continuity, WAPA/NERC scope, typical duration, and the specific NERC 10-minute correction standard -- no single document gives the complete picture.
- Confidence: high

This target was actually invoked when the July 2025 24-Month Study projected Lake Powell declining below the 3,525.00-ft DROA target elevation, triggering DROA year-2025 drought-response planning under the DCP-related agreements (EU-LP-OFF-022-03); consistent with protecting this same target, the December 2025 24-Month Study reduced Glen Canyon Dam's monthly release volumes for December 2025 through April 2026 by 0.598 maf (EU-LP-OFF-023-03).

## KC-LP-EMR-08 — Post-2012 High-Flow Experiment Protocol

- Operational question: What is the Glen Canyon Dam high-flow-experiment (HFE) protocol established since 2012, and what triggers its implementation?
- Engineering dimension: Emergency Operations
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: High flow experiments were tested in 1996, 1997, 2000, 2004, 2008, 2012, 2013, and 2014 to rebuild eroded sandbars; since 2012 a 10-year protocol implements one to two high flow experiments annually, timed to follow tributary sand inputs from the Paria River in fall and/or spring (EU-LP-RES-014-05). This decision-making protocol, approved by the Department of the Interior in 2012, coordinates dam releases to occur following large sand inputs delivered to the Colorado River by a major tributary (EU-LP-RES-018-01). Across the full historical record, 11 HFEs were released from Glen Canyon Dam between 1996 and November 2018, with peak magnitudes ranging from 864 to 1,274 cubic meters per second and durations from 24 to 168 hours, including lower-magnitude habitat maintenance flows (HMFs) in 1997 and 2000 (EU-LP-RES-018-02).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-RES-014-05`, `EU-LP-RES-018-01`, `EU-LP-RES-018-02`
- Source coverage: EU-LP-RES-014-05 gives the protocol's year-list and annual cadence; EU-LP-RES-018-01 gives the protocol's formal basis (2012 DOI approval, tributary-sand trigger logic); EU-LP-RES-018-02 supplies the full quantitative historical record of magnitudes and durations. EU-LP-RES-014-05 carries a source dimension tag of Real-Time Operations while the other two are tagged Emergency Operations; Emergency Operations is used here consistent with how HFEs are tagged elsewhere in this corpus.
- Confidence: high

## KC-LP-EMR-09 — 1996 First Controlled-Flood Experiment

- Operational question: What were the specifics and outcome of the 1996 Glen Canyon Dam controlled-flood (first HFE) experiment?
- Engineering dimension: Emergency Operations
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: In spring 1996 Reclamation conducted its first controlled-flood (beach/habitat-building) experiment, ramping releases from a steady 8,000 cfs baseline up to a peak of 45,000 cfs (approximately 1,274 cubic meters per second) held for 7 days beginning March 26, 1996, before stepping back down; preliminary results showed sandbars in the Glen and Grand canyons increased by as much as 30 percent and numerous new backwaters were created for fish (EU-LP-OFF-010-14). The 1996 HFE's major objective was to determine whether high releases from Glen Canyon Dam could effectively redistribute sand from the riverbed to the channel margins; because it followed two years of below-average tributary sand input, suspended sediment progressively coarsened and depleted during the event (EU-LP-RES-018-08).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-OFF-010-14`, `EU-LP-RES-018-08`
- Source coverage: A 1997 GAO report gives the release hydrograph and immediate sandbar/backwater results; a 2018 USGS research paper gives the experiment's stated objective and the antecedent sediment-supply context that constrained its outcome. Source dimension tags differ (EU-LP-OFF-010-14 tagged Real-Time Operations; EU-LP-RES-018-08 tagged Emergency Operations); Emergency Operations is used here consistent with HFE tagging elsewhere.
- Confidence: high

## KC-LP-EMR-13 — River Outlet Works: Capacity, History, and Reliability Risk

- Operational question: What are Glen Canyon Dam's River Outlet Works, what is their release capacity, and what is known about their historical use and reliability?
- Engineering dimension: Emergency Operations
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: The River Outlet Works (bypass tubes) have historically only been opened when the dam was first filled, during short-term high-flow experiments, and for emergency flood releases in the early 1980s, and have never been used over extended periods (EU-LP-CRS-002-03). Below minimum power pool, the River Outlet Works are the only remaining way to pass water through Glen Canyon Dam: two intakes leading to four 96-inch-diameter steel pipes with a combined maximum discharge capacity of 15,000 cubic feet per second (EU-LP-MED-002-02), a capacity figure confirmed on Reclamation's own project page, which also lists a combined spillway discharge capacity of 208,000 cfs at elevation 3,700.0 ft (EU-LP-OFF-030-02). Since the Draft SEIS, Reclamation identified enhanced risks associated with relying on the River Outlet Works as the exclusive means of releasing water at low reservoir elevations for sustained periods, prompting the 2024 Interim Operating Guidance for Low Reservoir Levels (EU-LP-OFF-007-04).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-CRS-002-03`, `EU-LP-MED-002-02`, `EU-LP-OFF-007-04`, `EU-LP-OFF-030-02`
- Source coverage: A CRS report, a journalism piece, a 2024 SEIS chapter, and Reclamation's own project page together establish the River Outlet Works' physical capacity, historically limited/short-duration use, and an emerging (2024) agency concern about relying on them for sustained low-elevation operation.
- Confidence: high

## KC-LP-EMR-14 — Hourly and Daily Dispatch Guidelines Within MLFF Constraints

- Operational question: What guidelines govern how Glen Canyon Dam's monthly release volumes are distributed across hours, days, and weeks in normal power operations?
- Engineering dimension: Real-Time Operations
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: Beyond formal ROD requirements, WAPA follows discretionary dispatch practices at Glen Canyon Dam -- such as holding nightly generation to the same minimum level during off-peak hours and keeping weekend releases at no less than 85 percent of the average weekday release -- that fall within MLFF constraints but are not ROD requirements and can be altered or abandoned by WAPA at any time (EU-LP-OFF-005-10). Per the 1997 GAO report, to the extent possible Glen Canyon Dam's hourly power operations followed three guidelines: maximize water releases during peak energy demand periods (generally Monday-Saturday, 7 a.m. to 11 p.m.); maximize releases during peak-demand months and minimize during low-demand months; and minimize or eliminate powerplant bypasses (EU-LP-OFF-010-13).
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-OFF-005-10`, `EU-LP-OFF-010-13`
- Source coverage: The 2016 LTEMP FEIS describes current discretionary WAPA dispatch practices; the 1997 GAO report describes the underlying hourly/monthly dispatch guidelines used to convert release volumes into a power-generation schedule. Together they characterize the day-to-day and hour-to-hour operational discretion available within MLFF constraints.
- Confidence: high

## KC-LP-MOD-01 — CRSS: Purpose, RiverWare Implementation, and Use in Planning Studies

- Operational question: What is the Colorado River Simulation System (CRSS) and what software platform implements it?
- Engineering dimension: Modeling
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: CRSS is a package of computer programs and databases, developed over roughly a 10-year period, designed to assist Reclamation in performing comprehensive long-range planning and operations studies of proposed changes to Colorado River operations (impacts on streamflows, floodflows, storage, water allocation deliveries, and basin yields). By 1996, CRSS was fully transitioned from a hardwired Fortran/Cyber mainframe model into the RiverWare software platform, preserving the original model's structure and data while allowing analysts to define and prioritize operating rules through a graphical interface instead of altering source code. In practice, CRSS/RiverWare has been used as one of a linked suite of simulation models (e.g., in the 2015 LTEMP decision analysis) specifically to forecast monthly water volumes and reservoir elevations feeding evaluation of long-term operating strategies.
- Supporting EUs: `EU-LP-OFF-010-07`, `EU-LP-OFF-021-01`, `EU-LP-RES-024-14`
- Source coverage: Three independent sources (1997 GAO EIS assessment, Post-2026 DEIS CRSS Model Documentation appendix, 2015 USGS LTEMP decision-analysis report) each contribute a different piece of the full picture (purpose, implementation platform, applied use); none alone states all three.
- Confidence: high

## KC-LP-MOD-05 — The 24-Month Study: Purpose and Operational Workflow

- Consolidation type: complementary_facts

## KC-LP-UNC-02 — Reliability Risk of the River Outlet Works as a Long-Term Release Path

- Operational question: Can the river outlet works (ROW) reliably serve as the sole long-term release mechanism for Glen Canyon Dam at low Lake Powell reservoir levels?
- Engineering dimension: Uncertainty and Risk Management
- Consolidation type: complementary_facts
- Consolidated finding: Multiple sources indicate the river outlet works (ROW) were not designed for, and cannot be fully relied upon for, long-term or high-flow releases at low Lake Powell levels. Reclamation's 2024 Technical Decision Memorandum found no design reference indicating the ROW were intended for long-term water conveyance at low reservoir levels; historically they were used only to augment powerplant/spillway discharges for short durations (e.g., about three months during 1983 flood releases, days to a week for high-flow experiments) (OFF-025-04).
