## Executive Summary

Lake Powell's WY2026 operations represent the most severe drought response in the reservoir's 60-year history. Every official source in this corpus — the USBR operations page, the May 2026 24-Month Study, the 2026 DROA Plan, and the CRSP Fact Sheet — converges on emergency operations language rather than standard tier-based management. *(SYN-LP-DEMO-001)*

The operational story has three layers: a **rules escalation** from standard tier baseline (7.48 maf) to unprecedented emergency reduction (6.0 maf); a **multi-lever emergency response** combining release cuts with upstream reservoir draws from Flaming Gorge; and an **unresolved infrastructure constraint** that limits how long the current strategy can hold without physical modifications to Glen Canyon Dam.

**Key projected outcome (most probable scenario):** Lake Powell ends WY2026 at approximately 3,510.85 ft with 4.77 maf in storage (20% of capacity) — 15 ft above minimum power pool and 25 ft below the DROA Target Elevation. *(KU-LP-DEMO-003)*

## 1. Operational Context

### 1.1 Hydrology

WY2026 inflow to Lake Powell is forecast at **3.27 maf** — 34% of the 1991–2020 average and among the lowest values since dam closure in 1963. The forecast range spans 2.95 maf (minimum probable, 31% of average) to 4.27 maf (maximum probable, 45% of average), leaving a 1.32 maf uncertainty window that directly bounds whether DROA measures will be sufficient. *(KU-LP-DEMO-004)*

The period 2000–2025 represents a structural shift in Upper Colorado Basin hydrology: a 23-year average of 8.29 maf, the lowest since dam closure, reflecting chronic aridification rather than a single anomalous year. Total Colorado Basin system storage at the start of WY2026 was approximately 21.8 maf — 37% of combined capacity. *(KU-LP-DEMO-004)*

### 1.2 Legal and Operational Foundation

Glen Canyon Dam is operated under the Colorado River Storage Project Act (1956) and governed by the "Law of the River" — a layered set of compacts, statutes, and agreements that constrain all release decisions. CRSP Initial Units have 30.6 maf of combined storage capacity. Approximately 75% of the CRSP Basin Fund revenue comes from Glen Canyon hydropower, creating a formal financial constraint on how release timing is managed. *(KU-LP-DEMO-011, KU-LP-DEMO-010)*

## 2. Rule Structure: The Three-Layer Escalation

The WY2026 release framework reflects three overlapping rule layers that activated in sequence. *(SYN-LP-DEMO-003, CKP-LP-DEMO-002)*

**Layer 1 — Standard Tier Setting:** The August 2025 24-Month Study projected Lake Powell's January 1, 2026 elevation between 3,525 ft and 3,575 ft, placing operations in the Mid-Elevation Release Tier under Section 6.C.1 of the Interim Guidelines. This set the standard annual release at **7.48 maf**. *(KU-LP-DEMO-001)*

**Layer 2 — DROA Activation:** When Lake Powell was projected to decline below 3,525 ft — the DROA Target Elevation — the Drought Response Operations Agreement was activated. DROA requires consideration of monthly volume adjustments at Glen Canyon Dam first, before upstream Initial Unit releases are drawn. *(KU-LP-DEMO-007, KU-LP-DEMO-009)*

**Layer 3 — Section 6.E Emergency Override:** Conditions deteriorated further, and the Department of Interior invoked Section 6.E emergency authority to reduce the annual release to **6.0 maf** — 1.48 maf below the tier baseline and unprecedented under Mid-Elevation Tier conditions. *(KU-LP-DEMO-002)*

> **Source note:** The 7.48 maf and 6.0 maf values are not contradictory — they represent two stages of the same escalating decision. *(SYN-LP-DEMO-005)*

**Structural constraint:** The operational tier is locked in annually by the August 24-Month Study. Once set, mid-year changes require invoking Section 6.E. This annual-locking mechanism means that deteriorating hydrology in the October–April window cannot change the release tier automatically — only emergency authority can. *(KU-LP-DEMO-006)*

## 4. Emergency Response: 2026 Drought Response Operations Plan

The 2026 DROA Plan, activated April 23, 2026, is the most extensive DROA deployment since the agreement was established. It combines three simultaneous measures: *(KU-LP-DEMO-008, SYN-LP-DEMO-003, CKP-LP-DEMO-004)*

1. **Release reduction to 6.0 maf** at Glen Canyon Dam — the legal minimum, never previously executed under the Mid-Elevation Tier.

2. **660,000 AF to 1.0 MAF of Flaming Gorge releases** (May 1, 2026 – April 30, 2027), with contingent additional releases from Blue Mesa (post-irrigation season) and Navajo Reservoir (Spring 2027 if available). The May 2026 24-Month Study formally incorporated the full 1.0 MAF as a model input. *(KU-LP-DEMO-005)*

3. **No anticipated recovery** of released volumes through the plan year — meaning the water transferred from Flaming Gorge will permanently reduce that reservoir's storage buffer within the plan period.

### 4.1 Reservoir Triage: A Finite Strategy

Reclamation's two-lever approach — hold more water in Powell, release more from Flaming Gorge — has been characterized by experts as "reservoir triage," deployable only "once or twice" before upstream buffers are exhausted. Flaming Gorge is projected to lose approximately 35 ft of elevation from the 2026 draws. *(KU-LP-DEMO-018)*

The DROA accounting system includes a credit/debit mechanism designed to restore water to Flaming Gorge over time — but the 2026 Plan explicitly anticipates no recovery, indicating that the hydrologic conditions for restoration are not expected within the plan year. *(KU-LP-DEMO-009, SYN-LP-DEMO-007)*

## 5. Infrastructure Constraint: The Unresolved Central Issue

The most consequential finding from the infrastructure sources is that Glen Canyon Dam's current plumbing is not capable of sustained operations at or below 3,500 ft. This creates a structural ceiling on how far current operational strategies can extend without physical modification. *(SYN-LP-DEMO-009, CKP-LP-DEMO-003)*

### 5.1 The Three Exit Pathways

Glen Canyon Dam has three water release pathways:

- **Spillways** at elevation 3,648 ft — not relevant at current or projected water levels.
- **Eight 15-ft diameter penstocks** — the primary release and hydropower pathway. Incapacitated below 3,490 ft (minimum power pool). *(KU-LP-DEMO-015)*
- **Four 8-ft diameter river outlet works** at elevation 3,370 ft — functionally limited at 3,394 ft, not designed for full-time use, and suffered cavitation damage during the 2023 high-flow experiment. *(KU-LP-DEMO-015)*

### 5.2 The 3,500 ft Practical Floor

Because Reclamation cannot confidently operate the river outlet works for extended periods, the agency maintains an operational focus on keeping Lake Powell above **3,500 ft** — 10 ft above the formal minimum power pool. This practical floor strands **4.4 MAF** of Lake Powell storage (only 3.7 MAF is technically accessible with current infrastructure). *(KU-LP-DEMO-016)*

> *Verification note: The 4.4 MAF and 3.7 MAF figures are reported in Circle of Blue (LP-DEMO-C-002) attributed to Reclamation analysis. Confirmation against official Reclamation technical documents is recommended before treating these as authoritative.*

### 5.3 Engineering Modification Timeline

Congress provided $2 million for an appraisal study in FY2022. Reclamation is assessing three options: (1) a new lower intake using existing turbines; (2) connecting power generation equipment to the river outlet works; and (3) tunneling through the canyon wall for a new underground power station. An appraisal study is expected in late 2026 or early 2027. Any action beyond the study requires congressional authorization; construction will take years. *(KU-LP-DEMO-017)*

**Implication:** No infrastructure fix is available before WY2027–2028 at the earliest. The DROA-based operational response must bridge this gap entirely. *(SYN-LP-DEMO-009)*

## 6. Governance Architecture

The coordination mechanisms governing WY2026 operations span four layers that are designed to activate sequentially but have all been deployed simultaneously: *(CKP-LP-DEMO-005, SYN-LP-DEMO-003)*

- **DROA** (Drought Response Operations Agreement) — formal legal instrument for upstream releases, with GCD monthly adjustments required before upstream draws.
- **Annual tier-locking** (August 24-Month Study) — sets the year's baseline, overridable only by Section 6.E emergency authority.
- **WAPA coordination** — Western Area Power Administration's power marketing obligations create a binding co-constraint on release timing; minimizing Basin Fund impacts is a DROA requirement, not a best-efforts goal. *(KU-LP-DEMO-010)*
- **DOI emergency authority** — independent of DROA; retained by the Department of Interior regardless of DROA status.

## 7. Research and Modeling Context

External research (LP-DEMO-B-002) confirms that approximately 80% of large US reservoirs use medium-to-long range inflow forecasts to inform release decisions, with western high-elevation systems using snowpack-based long-range horizons — validating the 24-Month Study's reliance on CBRFC snowpack forecasting as standard operational practice for systems like Lake Powell. *(KU-LP-DEMO-013, SYN-LP-DEMO-004)*

The same research finds that forecast-informed models outperform models neglecting forecast horizon, including during drought conditions. This raises an open question about whether CRSS (the model underlying the 24-Month Study) properly represents how forecast horizons shape release decisions — a potential systematic bias with implications for DROA activation timing. *(KU-LP-DEMO-014)*

## 8. Key Operational Tradeoffs

### Tradeoff 1: Storage protection vs. compact delivery vs. hydropower revenue *(SYN-LP-DEMO-006)*
Cutting releases to 6.0 maf protects Lake Powell elevation but reduces deliveries to downstream compact obligations and reduces Basin Fund hydropower revenue (75% Glen Canyon-dependent). These three objectives are simultaneously in tension. No single source acknowledges all three constraints at once.

### Tradeoff 2: Upstream DROA draws vs. future upstream resilience *(SYN-LP-DEMO-007)*
Deploying Flaming Gorge's storage to protect Lake Powell transfers drought risk upstream. The "no anticipated recovery" clause in the 2026 DROA Plan means Flaming Gorge's buffer is permanently reduced this year. The "once or twice" expert characterization suggests the system is within 1–2 iterations of exhausting this option.

## 10. Summary: The Unresolved Core Issue

The WY2026 Lake Powell case study reveals a convergent crisis with three time horizons:

**Immediate (WY2026):** Reservoir triage — release cuts to legal minimum + 1.0 MAF Flaming Gorge draw — holds Lake Powell above 3,500 ft at year-end (projected 3,510.85 ft). All official instruments activated simultaneously.

**Short-term (WY2027):** If hydrology does not recover, upstream DROA buffers (especially Flaming Gorge) may be unable to provide another 1.0 MAF draw without violating their own operational floors. Lake Mead is simultaneously declining toward its shortage thresholds, reducing system-wide flexibility.

**Medium-term (WY2027–2029):** Infrastructure modifications to Glen Canyon Dam are the structural solution, but require congressional authorization and multi-year construction. The appraisal study (expected late 2026/early 2027) is the next gating milestone. Until modifications are in service, operations remain bounded by the 3,500 ft practical floor with 4.4 MAF stranded below it.

The knowledge base synthesized in this demo run captures all three horizons through its 18 KUs and 9 synthesis cards, with explicit evidence gaps flagged for follow-up acquisition. *(SYN-LP-DEMO-009)*
